Academic Context

This project was developed during the Fishery Biology course as part of my MSc in Marine Biology. The work consisted of critically reviewing the scientific literature on one of the most fascinating discoveries in marine ecology: the offshore aggregation area known as the White Shark Café. The objective was to understand satellite telemetry, shark migration ecology, fisheries interactions, and conservation challenges in Areas Beyond National Jurisdiction (ABNJ), and to communicate these findings through an academic presentation.

The White Shark Café is a remote offshore aggregation area in the Pacific Ocean, located halfway between California and Hawaii. Discovered through satellite telemetry in the early 2000s, this region revealed that white sharks (Carcharodon carcharias) undertake extensive seasonal migrations far from coastal waters, challenging previous assumptions about their ecology and habitat use.

This project explored the scientific discoveries, ecological significance and conservation implications of the White Shark Café, synthesising evidence from peer-reviewed studies on satellite tagging, movement ecology, biodiversity assessments, and high seas conservation. All scientific results presented derive from published literature rather than original analyses conducted by me.

Scientific Background

Since the early 2000s, technological advances have enabled the production of sophisticated satellite tagging systems, revolutionising our understanding of marine animal movements, particularly for Protected, Endangered and Threatened (PET) species such as sharks.

Two main types of satellite tags have been instrumental in shark research:

PAT Tags

Pop-up Archival Tags are attached to the animal without capture, using a lance to insert a small dart into the musculature below the dorsal fin. They record light, pressure and temperature data, archiving information at predetermined intervals before detaching and floating to the surface for satellite transmission.

SPOT Tags

Smart Position/Only Temperature Transmission Tags are attached to the dorsal fin, requiring capture and fin perforation. They transmit real-time position and temperature data whenever the fin breaks the surface, providing high-resolution tracking over 1-2 years.

These technologies have revealed that eastern Pacific white sharks repeatedly migrate toward a previously unknown offshore aggregation area, challenging earlier assumptions that sharks remained primarily coastal. This discovery fundamentally changed our understanding of shark ecology, migratory behaviour and habitat connectivity.

Discovery of the White Shark Café

In the early 2000s, a team of researchers began tracking white shark populations in the North Pacific using PAT tags (Boustany et al., 2002). To their surprise, all tagged sharks along the California coast suddenly headed offshore, travelling thousands of kilometres to an area halfway between California and Hawaii.

This region, later named the White Shark Café or Shared Offshore Foraging Area (SOFA), revealed that sharks were not strictly coastal but spent significant time in the open ocean. During this migration, sharks changed their depth range from 0-30 metres to 100-300 metres, with occasional dives up to 500 metres. The migration was found to be seasonal and repeated annually.

Initially, researchers hypothesised that the area served as a foraging ground. However, satellite measurements of surface chlorophyll showed some of the lowest productivity levels in the entire Pacific, leading to the suggestion that the area was a "biological desert" (Jorgensen et al., 2012).

A 2018 research expedition led by Dr. Barbara Block, using modern technologies including ROVs, baited cameras, midwater trawls and eDNA analysis, revealed that despite low surface chlorophyll, the area supports extraordinary biodiversity, with over 110 vertebrate genera identified — making it a true open-ocean biodiversity hotspot (Schnetzer, 2022).

Fisheries Interaction

The White Shark Café is located in Areas Beyond National Jurisdiction (ABNJ), far from coastal waters and existing regulatory frameworks. This region, despite its remoteness, experiences significant fishing pressure, particularly from longline fisheries targeting tuna and swordfish.

The spatial overlap between shark aggregation areas and fishing effort creates substantial bycatch risk. White sharks, along with other pelagic species such as mako sharks and blue sharks, are frequently caught as bycatch in longline fisheries operating in the region. There is also evidence of illegal, unreported and unregulated (IUU) fishing targeting sharks for their fins and meat, which have high commercial value.

The impact of both bycatch and target fishing in aggregation areas with high biodiversity can lead to population declines, collapse and even extinction, particularly for species like sharks, which are highly vulnerable to overfishing due to their life history traits: long generation times, late maturity, long gestation periods and small litter sizes.

Conservation Implications

The White Shark Café is one of the areas involved in the High Seas Initiative, a programme aimed at full protection of critical offshore habitats. As an ABNJ, it lies beyond national Exclusive Economic Zones (EEZs), where fishing is poorly regulated despite being a biodiversity hotspot and an aggregation point for PET species.

Every year, an estimated 75 million tonnes of marine resources are harvested from ABNJs alone (excluding IUU fisheries) — equivalent to the weight of the entire global human population. This highlights the urgent need for international governance and conservation action in the high seas.

The High Seas Alliance, with support from UNESCO, is working to designate the White Shark Café as a Large Marine Protected Area (LMPA). LMPAs are MPAs established in ABNJs under the High Seas Treaty, developed under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), to protect and conserve biodiversity in these remote regions.

The White Shark Café meets the criteria for LMPA designation, as it represents an ecologically significant, remote and vulnerable area requiring international cooperation for effective conservation. Protecting offshore aggregation sites like this is critical for the survival of migratory species and for maintaining the ecological integrity of the high seas.
