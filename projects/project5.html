Academic Context

This page describes an academic presentation developed during my Master Degree in Marine Biology. The work consisted of critically reviewing and presenting published scientific literature rather than conducting original research. The objective was to analyze experimental evidence, understand the methodologies employed by the authors, and communicate the scientific findings effectively.

Project Overview

This academic project focused on the first documented case of facultative parthenogenesis in a cartilaginous fish, described in the seminal paper by Chapman et al. (2007) published in Biology Letters. The study reported a virgin birth in a captive female bonnethead shark (Sphyrna tiburo), providing the first genetically confirmed evidence of parthenogenesis in Chondrichthyes.

As part of my Master's coursework in Marine Evolutionary Biology, I critically analyzed the original publication, examined the experimental design, interpreted the molecular data, and presented the findings and their broader implications to the class. This exercise developed my ability to evaluate primary scientific literature, understand molecular genetics techniques, and communicate complex evolutionary concepts effectively.

The presentation covered the reproductive biology of sharks, the mechanisms of parthenogenesis, the molecular approaches used to confirm the event, and the evolutionary and conservation implications of this discovery.

Scientific Background

Sphyrna tiburo, commonly known as the bonnethead shark, is a small hammerhead species belonging to the family Sphyrnidae, order Carcharhiniformes. Its geographic distribution extends from the western Atlantic to the eastern Pacific, inhabiting shallow coastal waters from 0 to 90 metres depth, including sandy bottoms, seagrass meadows, coral reefs and estuaries.

This species exhibits placental viviparity, with litters of 4 to 12 pups and a gestation period of approximately 5 months. Sexual maturity is reached at 2 years for males and 2-3 years for females. The bonnethead is primarily durophagous, feeding mainly on crustaceans such as crabs, with a dentition adapted to crush hard prey (Wilga & Motta, 2000).

The study by Chapman et al. (2007) investigated an unexpected birth in a captive female bonnethead shark that had been isolated from males for over three years, raising questions about the reproductive mechanism involved.

Study Objectives

The original study by Chapman et al. (2007) aimed to determine the reproductive mechanism behind the birth of a viable female pup in a captive bonnethead shark maintained in the absence of males. Specifically, the authors tested three alternative hypotheses:

Sperm Storage

Evaluate whether the female had stored sperm from a previous mating event, potentially lasting several years.

Self-Fertilization

Assess the possibility of hermaphroditism or self-fertilization in the captive female.

Parthenogenesis

Test whether the offspring resulted from automictic parthenogenesis, a form of asexual reproduction.

Methodological Approach

In my review of the original study, I analyzed the molecular techniques employed by Chapman et al. (2007) to determine the origin of the offspring. The methodological approach I examined included:

Tissue Sampling

Collection of tissue samples from the three candidate mothers and the offspring for DNA extraction.

Microsatellite Genotyping

Analysis of four highly polymorphic microsatellite loci to genotype individuals and identify the mother.

Population Comparison

Comparison with genotypes from 119 wild S. tiburo to assess natural genetic diversity.

AFLP Fingerprinting

Multi-locus AFLP analysis to search for paternal-specific DNA fragments in the offspring's genome.

Main Results

The analysis of the published data revealed three key lines of evidence supporting parthenogenesis:

Microsatellite Analysis

The offspring was completely homozygous at all four microsatellite loci, carrying only maternal alleles. The probability of observing this level of homozygosity through sexual reproduction was p < 1 × 10⁻⁷.

AFLP Fingerprinting

Multi-locus AFLP analysis confirmed the absence of paternal-specific DNA fragments in the offspring, excluding the possibility of paternal contribution.

Sex Determination

The offspring was female, consistent with automictic parthenogenesis in a species with female homogamety (XX sex determination system).

Scientific Significance

This study represented a milestone in evolutionary biology by providing the first genetically confirmed evidence of parthenogenesis in Chondrichthyes. The findings demonstrated that facultative parthenogenesis occurs not only in reptiles, birds and fish, but also in cartilaginous fishes, expanding our understanding of reproductive plasticity in vertebrates.

The results have important evolutionary and conservation implications:

  • Reproductive plasticity — demonstrates that sharks, like other vertebrates, can switch between sexual and asexual reproduction when mates are unavailable.
  • Low-density populations — parthenogenesis may serve as a reproductive "safety net" in overexploited or fragmented populations where finding mates is challenging.
  • Conservation biology — highlights the need to consider asexual reproduction in conservation strategies for endangered shark species.
  • Automictic mechanism — the study confirmed automictic parthenogenesis, where offspring are not perfect clones but show reduced genetic diversity due to increased homozygosity.

The discovery also raised important questions about the frequency and adaptive significance of parthenogenesis in wild shark populations, particularly in the context of overfishing and habitat degradation (Fields et al., 2015; Van der Kooi & Schwander, 2015).
